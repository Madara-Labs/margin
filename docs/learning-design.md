# Margin — Learning Design & Research Foundations

## Core Design Principle: The Book Stays in the Middle

Most AI reading tools invert the cognitive division of labor: they read, summarize, and map the text for the user, reducing the human to a passive consumer of fluent digests. Cognitive psychology and learning-science literature shows that this optimizes for the *illusion of competence* while stripping away the generative effort that produces durable understanding and transfer.

**Margin** is built on the opposite rule: **the reader's attempt comes first; AI output comes second, as a comparison.** There is no chat-with-your-PDF box as the main interface, and summaries are never shown on open. Instead, the software builds anchored study instruments in the margins and between sections—pre-questions, free-recall comparisons, memory concept-map canvases, Socratic self-explanation prompts, and verified interactive mathematical labs—that require the reader to predict, retrieve, derive, and enact before seeing a reference comparison.

## Detailed Research Grounding

### 1. Why Margin Never Auto-Summarizes on Open (Dunlosky et al., 2013)
In their comprehensive monograph reviewing ten popular study techniques across cognitive and educational psychology, **Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan, and Willingham (2013)** evaluated each strategy across learning conditions, student characteristics, materials, and criterion tasks:
- **Low Utility:** *Re-reading*, *highlighting/underlining*, and *summarization* (especially passive consumption of pre-written summaries) received low utility ratings. When software auto-summarizes a chapter or highlights key sentences for the reader, it automates the lowest-utility activities and removes desirable difficulties.
- **High Utility:** *Practice testing* (retrieval practice) and *distributed practice* (spaced revisits) received the highest utility rating across complex, criterion-level comprehension tasks.
- **Moderate Utility:** *Self-explanation*, *elaborative interrogation* (asking *"why is this true?"*), and *interleaved practice* received moderate ratings and are particularly effective for mathematically dense texts with causal derivations like Bolton & Dewatripont's *Contract Theory*.

### 2. Whole-Section Free Recall and Memory Concept Maps vs. Isolated Flashcards (Karpicke & Blunt, 2011)
Flashcard systems (such as Anki) atomize knowledge into isolated cue-response fragments, stripping away the deductive chain connecting assumptions, constraints (IR and IC), and equilibrium outcomes. **Karpicke and Blunt (2011)** (*Science*, "Retrieval Practice Produces More Learning than Elaborative Studying with Concept Mapping") demonstrated two critical findings that directly shape Methods 2 and 3 in Margin:
1. **Free recall beats open-book concept mapping:** Students who read a scientific text and then practiced free recall ("write down everything you remember with the text closed") outperformed students who constructed elaborate concept maps *while viewing the text*, even when the final criterion test was constructing a concept map.
2. **Concept mapping *from memory* is a potent form of retrieval practice:** Subsequent work by Karpicke and colleagues showed that when learners construct a concept map *with the book closed* (retrieving both nodes and relational links from memory) and then inspect feedback, they gain both the retrieval effect and structural organization. In Margin, the reader's page blurs during Method 2 (Free Recall) and the reference concept map in Method 3 (Retrieval Map) cannot be revealed until the reader has drawn their own nodes and arrows from memory.

### 3. Generative Learning Only Works When the Learner Generates (Fiorella & Mayer, 2015)
**Fiorella and Mayer (2015)** (*Learning as a Generative Activity: Eight Learning Strategies that Promote Understanding*) synthesized decades of empirical research on the SOI (*Select–Organize–Integrate*) model across eight generative strategies: *summarizing, mapping, drawing, imagining, self-testing, self-explaining, teaching,* and *enacting*. Across all eight strategies, learning gains depend on the learner actively selecting relevant material, organizing it into a coherent structure, and integrating it with prior knowledge. If an AI generates the summary, draws the concept map upfront, or explains the derivation first, the generative step is bypassed. Margin enforces this boundary:
- **Summarising:** Gated behind `.summary-gate-details` in `FreeRecallPanel.tsx`—the reference synthesis unlocks only after the reader writes their own summary attempt.
- **Enacting:** Realized in `ExplorableLab.tsx` (Method 5), where the reader manipulates model parameters (`c`, `η`, `σ`, `w̄`) and observes real-time dual-coded readouts and curves.
- **Teaching & Imagining:** Realized in Method 8 (Teach-Back to a confused novice that never explains) and Method 9 (Transfer prompts anchored to the reader's pinned project).

### 4. Pretesting and Productive Failure (Sinha & Kapur, 2021)
**Sinha and Kapur (2021)** (*Review of Educational Research*, "When Problem Solving Followed by Instruction Works: Evidence for Productive Failure") meta-analyzed 53 empirical studies comparing problem-solving/attempt-first designs against instruction-first designs. Generating attempts, hypotheses, or predictions *before* being shown the canonical solution significantly enhances conceptual understanding and transfer (with high fidelity to Productive Failure principles yielding strong positive effect sizes). Margin embeds this in two places:
- **Method 1 (Pre-questions):** Before reading §4.2, the reader commits a one-line guess to 2–3 conceptual questions. As they read down the page, their exact guess resurfaces in the right margin beside the paragraph that answers it (`p-4.2-5`, `p-4.2-7`, `p-4.2-9`), creating an immediate comparison moment.
- **Method 5 (Predict-First Sliders):** Before any parameter slider change in the Explorable Lab takes effect, Margin asks the reader to commit a directional prediction (`↑ Goes Up`, `↓ Goes Down`, `= Stays Same`) for Second-Best Profit $\Pi^{SB}$, then immediately compares their prediction against the evaluated math model.
